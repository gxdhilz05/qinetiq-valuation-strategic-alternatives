# WACC, terminal value and DCF assumptions

## What the DCF is doing

The operating forecast produces unlevered free cash flow (UFCF), which is cash available to both lenders and shareholders before interest payments. Because the cash flow is pre-financing, it is discounted at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which blends the return required by equity investors with the after-tax cost of debt.

```text
Cost of equity = Risk-free rate + Relevered beta x Equity risk premium
After-tax cost of debt = Pre-tax cost of debt x (1 - tax rate)
WACC = Equity weight x Cost of equity + Debt weight x After-tax cost of debt
```

The present value of FY27-FY31 UFCF plus the present value of the terminal value equals enterprise value. Net debt is then deducted to obtain equity value.

```text
Terminal value = FY31 UFCF x (1 + perpetual growth) / (WACC - perpetual growth)
Enterprise value = PV of forecast UFCF + PV of terminal value
Equity value = Enterprise value - net debt
```

## Base-case WACC assumptions

| Input | Assumption | Rationale |
|---|---:|---|
| UK 10-year gilt yield | 5.39% | Sterling nominal risk-free proxy observed on 16 September 2026. |
| Equity risk premium | 4.14% | Latest implied mature-market equity risk premium published by Aswath Damodaran at the research date. |
| Aerospace/Defence unlevered beta | 0.85x | Sector beta is more stable than QinetiQ's standalone observed beta; January 2026 sector dataset. |
| Market capitalisation | £2,434.72m | Current London Stock Exchange instrument market capitalisation at the research date. |
| Gross debt | £333.6m | FY26 term loan; used for capital-structure weighting and relevering beta. |
| Cash tax rate | 26.5% | Same normalized rate used in the UFCF forecast. |
| Pre-tax cost of debt | 5.0% | Normalized estimate based on the 3.46% weighted-average fixed swap rate, the 1.00% term-loan margin and the floating portion of debt. |

These inputs produce a relevered beta of approximately 0.94x, cost of equity of approximately 9.3%, after-tax cost of debt of approximately 3.7%, and WACC of approximately 8.6%.

The model uses market value for equity and book value for debt because listed equity is continuously repriced, while the short-dated bank term loan is a reasonable proxy for its market value. The current market inputs are a WACC calibration snapshot; the forecast itself begins from the last audited year end, 31 March 2026.

## Terminal value and discounting assumptions

| Input | Assumption | Rationale |
|---|---:|---|
| Perpetual growth rate | 2.0% | Consistent with the Bank of England's long-term inflation target and conservative for a mature nominal business. It is below WACC, as required mathematically and economically. |
| Discounting convention | Mid-year | UFCF is earned through the year rather than entirely on the final day. FY27 is discounted by 0.5 years, FY28 by 1.5 years, through FY31 by 4.5 years. |
| Terminal cash flow | FY31 UFCF | FY31 is the final explicit forecast year and is grown once by the perpetual growth rate in the Gordon Growth formula. |
| Net debt | £159.1m | FY26 company-defined net debt deducted in the enterprise-to-equity bridge. |
| Shares outstanding | 522.1m | Shares in issue at 31 March 2026, net of treasury shares. |

At the base assumptions, the model indicates enterprise value of approximately £3.0bn and equity value of approximately £2.9bn, or roughly £5.5 per share. The workbook calculates the exact amounts from the forecast rather than hardcoding them.

## Interview explanation

“I forecast UFCF because it measures cash generated by the operations before financing, so it belongs to both debt and equity investors. I therefore discount it at WACC, not the cost of equity. I estimated cost of equity with CAPM, using a UK gilt yield, a sector beta relevered to QinetiQ's capital structure and an implied equity risk premium. I estimated debt cost from the company's term-loan pricing and applied the tax shield. For cash flows beyond FY31 I used the Gordon Growth method with 2% perpetual growth, below WACC and consistent with a mature nominal economy. I used mid-year discounting because cash arrives throughout the year. The resulting present values give enterprise value, from which I deduct net debt to reach equity value.”
