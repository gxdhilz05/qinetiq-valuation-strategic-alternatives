# QinetiQ Company Research

## Company overview

QinetiQ is a defence and security technology company. It earns money by providing:

- Engineering services
- Testing and evaluation of missiles and aircraft
- Military training
- Mission support
- Intelligence and cyber operations
- Research and development

In FY26, approximately 96% of QinetiQ's revenue came from service contracts. [S006]

### FY26 revenue by capability

| Capability | Revenue share |
|---|---:|
| Engineering services | 34% |
| Mission support and operations | 27% |
| Test and training | 26% |
| Research and development | 13% |

This mix reinforces that QinetiQ is primarily a specialist services and technology business rather than a major platform manufacturer. [S006]

## History

QinetiQ originated from DERA (the Defence Evaluation and Research Agency) in 2001. When DERA was divided into QinetiQ and Dstl, highly sensitive activities remained inside government and became Dstl. Most of the remaining activities became QinetiQ, which was subsequently privatised. In 2006, it was listed on the London Stock Exchange. [S005]

Because it originated from DERA, QinetiQ inherited specialist technical employees such as researchers and scientists, prior security knowledge and clearances, experience working with the MOD, established customer relationships and responsibilities connected with important testing facilities.

## Why customers use QinetiQ

Military systems need to be tested safely, and governments require trusted, security-cleared suppliers. QinetiQ has specialist scientists and engineers and access to infrastructure that would be expensive to reproduce. Replacing it with another provider could therefore create technical and security risks.

The advantage of these customers is that governments can offer large, long-term contracts and have reliable access to funding.

The risk is that QinetiQ is highly dependent on government priorities, procurement decisions and contract timing.

If a government changed its spending plans, QinetiQ would not immediately lose all its revenue because it already has signed contracts and funded backlog. However, future orders and longer-term growth could weaken. [S006]

## Long Term Partnering Agreement (LTPA)

The LTPA is an important agreement between QinetiQ and the MOD under which:

- QinetiQ delivers test, trials, training and evaluation services.
- Work is conducted across 16 core MOD-owned sites.
- The original framework began in 2003.
- In May 2025, it was extended for five years.

The extension was worth approximately £1.54bn. QinetiQ's FY26 order intake also included £166m of associated investment, producing the larger reported figure of approximately £1.7bn. [S003, S004]

The LTPA is important because it provides:

- Revenue visibility
- A strong MOD relationship
- Access to specialist facilities
- A significant barrier to competitors

The facilities are generally MOD-owned rather than QinetiQ-owned.

## How QinetiQ's contracts become revenue

**Opportunity → contract award → order intake → funded backlog → work performed → revenue → cash**

### Order intake

New contracted work booked during the year.

### Funded backlog

Contracted work that has customer funding but has not yet been recognised as revenue.

Backlog is not automatically revenue, profit or cash because QinetiQ must still perform the work, employees and facilities create costs, different contracts have different margins, delivery can be delayed, and contracts can be changed or cancelled.

### Revenue

Revenue is recorded as QinetiQ performs its contractual obligations.

### Cash

Cash may be collected before or after revenue is recognised.

At the end of FY26, QinetiQ had £4.421bn of funded backlog. Approximately £1.347bn was expected to become FY27 revenue, with the remainder scheduled for later years. [S004, S006]

Reported FY26 order intake was £3.573bn because it included the unusually large LTPA extension. Management's adjusted book-to-bill was 1.14x. We should use the adjusted measure when judging recurring demand so that one major contract extension does not distort the trend. [S001, S004]

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 1,320.4 | 1,580.7 | 1,912.1 | 1,931.6 | 1,922.6 |

| Segment operating margin | 10.4% | 11.3% | 11.3% | 9.6% | 11.3% |

| Net cash/(debt) | 225.1 | (206.9) | (151.2) | (133.2) | (159.1) |

Revenue grew strongly between FY22 and FY24, with some growth coming from acquisitions, especially in the US. Group revenue then flattened, and FY25 profitability was damaged by the US operation.

FY26 profit recovered because margins improved. Free cash flow was approximately £159m, while net leverage remained relatively low at around 0.5x EBITDA.

From FY25 to FY26, EMEA revenue increased and its margin improved. Global Solutions revenue decreased, but its margin still improved.

Group revenue therefore declined slightly while operating profit increased because QinetiQ:

- Reduced costs
- Sold Federal IT
- Exited lower-return work
- Restructured the US operation

These actions reduced revenue but improved profitability. [S001, S004, S006]

## Underlying versus statutory performance

QinetiQ presents both statutory results, which follow accounting standards, and underlying performance, which shows management's view of the core ongoing business.

In FY25, underlying segment operating profit was £185.4m, while the statutory operating result was a £90.5m loss. A major reason for the difference was an impairment associated with the US business. This meant the accounting value of the business was reduced because management no longer expected it to generate as much value as originally forecast.

Although the impairment was non-cash in that year, it remains important because it suggests that previous acquisition expectations were too optimistic. [S004, S006]

## US underperformance

The US operation has experienced:

- Slower contract awards
- Budget and procurement uncertainty
- Changes in government spending priorities
- Weak contract execution
- Lower-return work
- Previous acquisition impairments

Management has responded by:

- Selling Federal IT
- Cutting costs
- Exiting lower-return contracts
- Restructuring the operation
- Beginning a strategic review

US profitability improved in FY26, but revenue fell. Therefore, we cannot yet conclude that the business has achieved a genuine operational recovery. [S001, S002, S004]

## Strategic alternatives

### 1. Retain the US business

This would keep access to the US defence market, preserve a potential future recovery, avoid selling at a depressed valuation and maintain geographical diversification.

However, QinetiQ would retain execution risk, the possibility of further impairments, and the use of management time and capital. Continued US weakness could also depress QinetiQ's overall valuation.

### 2. Retain and restructure further

This could improve margins before a future sale, allow QinetiQ to exit poor contracts and create greater value if the turnaround succeeds.

However, there would be additional restructuring costs, recovery could take several years, there is no guarantee that revenue or orders would recover, and investors may remain sceptical.

### 3. Sell the US business

Removing the weaker operation would release cash, simplify the group, allow management to focus on EMEA and potentially reveal the value of the higher-quality core business.

However, selling during a weak period might produce a low price. QinetiQ would lose potential future US growth, tax and transaction costs would reduce proceeds, and a low sale price could confirm that previous acquisitions destroyed value.

The best option will be the one offering the strongest combination of shareholder value, strategic logic, risk, cash generation and execution feasibility.

## Commercial awareness

Higher UK and allied defence budgets increase the potential amount of work available. However, an increase in defence spending does not necessarily mean QinetiQ's revenue will increase. Money must be allocated to relevant programmes, QinetiQ must win the contracts, and it must then perform the work.

Growing technological complexity in autonomous systems, drones, cyber and electronic warfare should increase demand for testing, assurance, training and systems integration.

However, government contracting can be slow. Delayed funding or approvals can delay orders, revenue and cash collection.

Labour shortages and wage inflation could delay delivery, increase costs and reduce margins because QinetiQ relies on specialist, security-cleared employees.

Higher interest rates can increase interest expense on floating-rate debt, increase WACC and reduce DCF valuation.

QinetiQ reports in pounds but earns revenue in US and Australian dollars. Currency movements can therefore change reported financial results even when the underlying local operations have not changed. We must distinguish between reported growth, organic growth and constant-currency growth.

### Capital allocation

QinetiQ can use cash for organic investment, acquisitions, debt repayment, dividends or share buybacks. The previous US impairments mean acquisition discipline must be assessed rather than assumed. Buybacks create value per share only if the shares are repurchased below intrinsic value and the company is not sacrificing a better use of the cash.

## Current FY27 guidance

Management currently expects:

- Revenue growth of 3%–5%
- An underlying operating margin of 11.0%–11.5%
- Underlying EPS growth of 8%–10%
- Cash conversion above 90%

These are benchmarks to test rather than assumptions we should automatically copy into the model. [S002]

## Key catalysts and risks

Potential catalysts include a favourable US strategic-review outcome, major contract wins, successful backlog conversion, sustained EMEA margins, strong cash conversion and value-creating share buybacks.

The main risks are UK government concentration, procurement delays, contract cost overruns, further US impairments, a disappointing US sale price, labour-cost pressure, weak cash conversion and poor future acquisitions.

## KPIs to monitor

The model and strategic review will focus on:

- EMEA and Global Solutions revenue growth
- Segment operating margins
- Order intake and adjusted book-to-bill
- Funded backlog and its conversion into revenue
- Cash conversion and free cash flow
- Net debt and leverage
- US contract awards, restructuring costs and further impairments
- Share count and the price paid for buybacks

## Why QinetiQ is suitable for this project

QinetiQ has enough public information to build a proper model, two segments with contrasting performance and a live strategic decision concerning the US business. It can therefore be assessed using a DCF, trading comparables, sum-of-the-parts valuation and strategic scenarios. It is also less generic than analysing a large platform manufacturer.

My interest in aeronautical engineering led me to become interested in the testing, systems-engineering and mission-support activities in which QinetiQ is involved.

More importantly, QinetiQ has a live corporate-finance problem: a strong and visible EMEA business alongside an underperforming US operation under strategic review.
